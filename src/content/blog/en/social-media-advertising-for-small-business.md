## What is social media advertising, and how is it different from posting?

Posting is **organic**: you publish, and your followers (plus a few other people) see it. Advertising is **paid**: you pay a platform to show a specific message to a specific audience, and you choose the goal, such as phone calls, form fills, messages or online purchases.

Posting builds presence over time. Ads buy attention now. They work well together, but they are different jobs. If you need customers this month, ads are faster. If your profile looks abandoned, though, ads will just send people to a dead page.

## Which platform should a small business advertise on?

Start where your customers already spend time, not where you do.

- **Facebook and Instagram (Meta):** the usual first stop for local services, restaurants, retail, beauty and home services. One ad system covers both, with solid targeting by location, age and interests.
- **TikTok:** worth testing if your product is visual, you sell to younger buyers and you can produce short video regularly.
- **LinkedIn:** for B2B and high-ticket services. Clicks are expensive, so it only pays off if one new client is worth thousands of dollars.
- **YouTube:** good for products that need explaining, but video production is a real cost.
- **Pinterest:** useful for home, weddings, crafts and food.

One honest caveat: if people already search for what you sell (“emergency plumber near me”), search ads can beat social ads, because social **interrupts** people while search **answers** them. Social ads shine when people don’t know they need you yet, or when your offer is visual or impulse-friendly.

**Rule of thumb:** one platform, one goal, 60 to 90 days. Spreading a small budget across four platforms teaches you nothing.

## How much should a small business spend on social media ads?

There are three separate costs, and they get mixed up all the time:

1. **Ad spend:** the money you pay the platform. Many small businesses start at roughly $300 to $1,500 a month. Much less than that can make it hard to gather enough data. Competitive local markets may need more.
2. **Creative:** photos, video and copy. You can start with your own photos and a phone video. You don’t need a studio, but you do need images that look real and clear.
3. **Management:** if someone builds and optimizes the campaigns for you. Market rates vary widely, typically from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars a month, either flat or as a percentage of spend. Treat that as a rough range, not a quote.

Decide in advance how much you are willing to spend to **learn** what works. Then run long enough to learn it. The platform bills you directly, so the ad spend should never be hidden inside an agency invoice.

## What do you need before you spend the first dollar?

Ads amplify what you already have. If the pieces behind them are weak, you pay more to find that out.

1. **A place to send people.** A fast landing page or website that loads well on a phone, says what you do in one sentence and has one clear action (call, book, request a quote). Sending ad traffic to a homepage with twelve menu items leaks leads. If you need that page, see our [web design service](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en).
2. **A clear offer.** “Free estimate this week” or “first class free” beats “we are the best”.
3. **Tracking.** Conversion tracking on your site and a way to count calls or messages. Without it, you are guessing.
4. **A fast response.** Leads go cold in hours. If messages arrive at night or on weekends, an automatic first reply and a simple booking flow can save them.
5. **A profile that looks alive.** People will click your name. A few recent posts and real photos build trust.

## What goal should your first campaign have?

Pick one outcome that maps to money:

- **Service business:** leads (form fills, calls or messages).
- **Online store:** purchases.
- **Local shop or restaurant:** store visits, orders or reservations.
- **Brand-new business:** a modest awareness phase is fine, but only for a short time, and followed by a lead or sales campaign.

Avoid goals that reward vanity numbers, like post likes or video views, unless awareness is truly the point.

## How do you set up your first campaign?

1. **Define the customer.** Who are they, where do they live, what problem do they have right now?
2. **Choose one goal** from the list above.
3. **Set the audience simply.** Start with location and a broad age range. Over-narrow targeting often raises costs and limits learning.
4. **Make three or four ad versions.** Change the image or the opening line, one idea per ad.
5. **Write plainly.** What you offer, who it’s for, what to do next. No jargon.
6. **Set a daily budget and a run time.** Then leave it alone. Editing every day resets learning and muddies results.
7. **Review weekly.** Pause the weakest ad, keep the best and test one new idea at a time.

## How do you know if your ads are working?

Measure **cost per result**, not likes.

Here is an illustrative example (made-up numbers): you spend $600 and get 20 leads, so each lead costs $30. If one in four leads becomes a customer worth $400, you pay about $120 to earn $400. Your real numbers will differ, but the math works the same way.

Also watch:

- **Click-through rate:** are people interested in the ad?
- **Landing page conversion:** do clicks turn into leads?
- **Response time:** how fast do you answer?

If you get plenty of clicks but few leads, the problem is usually the page or the offer, not the ad.

## What mistakes waste the most ad money?

- Boosting posts with no goal.
- Sending all traffic to the homepage.
- Changing everything every two days.
- Targeting an audience so narrow the platform can’t learn.
- Not following up on leads quickly.
- Judging success by likes and comments.
- Letting an agency run ads from **their** ad account, so you lose the data when you leave.
- Skimping on creative while paying for clicks.

## Should you run ads yourself or hire someone?

Run them yourself if the budget is small, you have a few hours a week and your offer is simple. Hire help if the spend is growing, you want more than one platform or you don’t have the time to check results weekly.

Questions to ask anyone you consider:

- **Who owns the ad account, the pixel and the data?** It should be you.
- **How do you define a result** for my business?
- **What will you report, and how often?**
- **What is your fee, separate from ad spend?**
- **What do you need from me** (photos, offers, fast replies)?
- **What happens if results are weak after 90 days?**
