For many owners, social media becomes one more thing that slips. Posts go out in bursts, then nothing for three weeks. Messages sit unanswered. You suspect it matters, but you can't tell whether paying someone would fix the problem or just add a bill.

Here is how to decide without guessing.

## What does a social media manager actually do?

A good one does much more than hit publish. The usual scope looks like this:

- **Plans a content calendar** around your offers, seasons, and goals.
- **Creates or edits content**: captions, photos, graphics, short videos.
- **Publishes on a schedule** so your accounts never go quiet.
- **Replies to comments and messages**, sometimes. Confirm this, because it's often not included.
- **Reports on what's working**, ideally in terms of messages, calls, or bookings, not just likes.
- **Runs paid campaigns**, usually as a separate service with a separate ad budget.

What a manager can't do: fix a weak offer, make up for a website that doesn't convert, or know your business without input from you. They need your photos, your stories, and your answers to customer questions.

## Should I hire a social media manager or do it myself?

This is the question that matters. Use these signals.

**It's probably time to hire if:**

- You've tried to post consistently for months and keep falling behind.
- Your time is worth more somewhere else, like serving customers or closing sales.
- Customers already find you or check you out on Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok before they call.
- You have something visual or useful to show: finished jobs, products, people, results.
- You can budget for at least three to six months. Results build slowly.

**It's probably too early if:**

- You have no clear offer or can't say who your customer is.
- You have no website or booking page for people to land on.
- Nobody is assigned to answer the leads that come in.
- You've never posted yourself, so you can't brief anyone on what works.
- Your budget covers one month and then stops.

A quick test: write down in one sentence what you want social media to do for your business. *Bring in two new appointment requests a week* is a goal. *Be more present* is not. If you can't write the sentence yet, hiring will only hide the problem.

## What are my options if I hire?

There are four common setups:

1. **Freelancer.** Usually the most affordable. Quality varies, and one person covers everything, so ask about backup when they're out.
2. **Agency or creative studio.** Costs more, but you get a team: strategy, design, copy, sometimes video.
3. **Part-time employee or assistant.** Works if you want someone in your business daily, but you manage them and supply the direction.
4. **Hybrid.** You capture photos and short clips on your phone, and someone else plans, edits, and publishes. It's a very good fit for service businesses and restaurants.

The right choice depends on how much content you can provide and how much management you want to do yourself.

## How much does a social media manager cost?

Prices vary a lot by city, scope, and experience, so treat these as **rough market ranges**, not quotes:

- **Freelancers** serving small businesses often charge from a few hundred dollars to a couple thousand dollars a month.
- **Agencies and studios** commonly start around $1,000 to $1,500 a month and can climb to several thousand, depending on content volume and video.
- **Paid ads** are on top: the manager's fee for running them, plus the ad budget itself.

Very cheap packages usually mean posting only, with templates and no strategy or replies. That's fine if you just need a steady presence. It's not fine if you expect leads.

## What should I have ready before hiring?

A manager works faster and costs you less when you bring:

- **One clear goal** (calls, bookings, store visits, online orders).
- **A description of your ideal customer** and your best-selling services.
- **Brand basics**: logo, colors, a few photos you like.
- **A content source**: who takes photos, who approves posts, how fast.
- **A plan for messages**: who answers, how quickly, and what happens with a lead. If you answer the same questions all day, some of that can be handled with [custom automation](/servicios/desarrollo-de-software/?lang=en) so replies don't depend on you.
- **A place to send people**: a website or landing page that makes it easy to contact you. See our [web design](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) work if yours needs help.

## What should I ask before signing?

- **Who will actually do the work?** The person who sells you or someone else?
- **Can I see accounts you've managed**, and talk to a current client?
- **What exactly is included?** Number of posts, videos, stories, replies, reports.
- **Who owns the accounts?** They should be in your name, with you as admin.
- **How do approvals work?** And what happens when something urgent comes up?
- **How will you measure success** in terms of my business, not just followers?
- **What's the contract length**, and how do I exit?

If an answer is vague, that tells you something.

## What mistakes should I avoid?

- **Hiring for follower count.** Followers who never buy don't pay your bills.
- **Handing over your password** and losing admin access.
- **Expecting results in two weeks.**
- **Hiring without providing content.** A manager can't invent your jobs, products, or customers.
- **Ignoring the inbox.** Posts attract attention, and unanswered messages waste it.
- **Paying for fake followers or engagement**, which hurts reach and credibility.

## What if I'm still not sure?

Start small. Post yourself for four to six weeks, note what gets responses, and then hire for a defined trial of three months with clear goals. You'll brief better, judge better, and avoid paying for a learning curve.
