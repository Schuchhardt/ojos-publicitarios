A good social media manager saves you hours and keeps your business visible. A bad one burns budget, posts generic content, and sometimes ends up holding your accounts hostage. Trust doesn't come from a nice pitch. It comes from a few things you can check.

## What does a social media manager actually do?

The title covers very different jobs. Before you talk to anyone, know which one you are buying:

- **Posting only:** someone publishes content that you provide.
- **Content and posting:** they plan, write, design, and publish.
- **Full management:** the above, plus replying to comments and messages, reporting, and adjusting the plan.
- **Paid campaigns:** running ads. This is a separate skill and a separate budget, because the ad spend goes to the platform, not to the manager.

Most small businesses need the second or third. If a proposal doesn't say which one it is, ask.

## Do you need to hire someone at all?

Hire help when you know social media matters for your business but you keep dropping it because you are busy. That is the most common and most legitimate reason.

Wait if any of these are true:

- You can't yet say in one sentence what you sell and to whom.
- You have nowhere to send people. Social posts without a website, booking link, or a clear way to contact you waste attention. If that's your case, fix that first with a [simple, well-built website](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en).
- You expect sales in two weeks. Organic social media is a steady, slow channel.

## What should you have ready before you start looking?

You will get better proposals, and spot weak ones faster, if you can answer these:

1. **What is the goal?** Calls, bookings, store visits, online orders, or simply staying visible. Pick one main goal.
2. **Which platforms matter?** Where do your customers actually spend time? Not all of them.
3. **What is the budget range?** Even a rough monthly number helps.
4. **What can you provide?** Photos, product details, stories, approvals. Content about a real business needs input from the owner.

## Freelancer, agency, or employee?

There is no universal answer. Here is how to think about it:

- **Freelancer:** usually the lowest cost and the most personal. The risk is dependence on one person, and what happens when they are sick or busy.
- **Agency or studio:** typically more expensive, but covers design, copywriting, and continuity. The risk is being treated as a small account.
- **In-house employee:** only makes sense when there is enough work for a real role and you want to manage someone day to day.

As a very rough market range, light freelance help can start at a few hundred dollars a month, while fuller management with design and reporting often runs into the low thousands. Treat those as approximate and ask for a written quote.

## How do you check that someone is trustworthy?

Look for evidence you can verify, not adjectives.

- **Real accounts they have handled.** Visit them. Look at the quality, consistency, and whether the replies to comments sound human.
- **Work similar to yours.** They don't need your exact industry, but they should understand local or small-business customers.
- **A clear process.** Who plans, who approves, how many posts, how many revisions, what the calendar looks like.
- **Honest answers about results.** Anyone who guarantees followers, viral posts, or a fixed number of sales is selling smoke.
- **They ask you questions.** A good manager wants to understand your customers, your offer, and your tone before proposing anything.

## What should you ask before you sign?

Bring this list to the conversation:

- What exactly is included each month, and what costs extra?
- Who creates the content, and can I see samples before we start?
- How and how often do I approve posts?
- Who answers comments and direct messages, and how fast?
- What will you report, and how often? Which numbers matter for my goal?
- Who owns the accounts and any content created?
- Is there a minimum contract? How do I cancel?
- What do you need from me each week or month?

The answers matter, but so does how they answer. Clear and simple is a good sign. Vague or defensive is not.

## What red flags should you watch for?

- **They want your passwords.** Access should be through each platform's permissions, never shared logins.
- **They want to create the account in their name.** Your business profiles must belong to you.
- **Guaranteed results.** Nobody controls algorithms.
- **Buying followers or likes.** It looks good in a report and does nothing for your business.
- **Generic content.** If the sample posts could belong to any business, they will be about yours too.
- **No reporting.** You should always know what was published and what happened.
- **Pressure to sign immediately.** A reliable provider can wait a few days.

## How should access and ownership work?

This is where most bad stories begin, so be strict about it.

- **You create or keep the accounts** under your own email and your own business profile.
- **You stay the owner or main admin.** Give the manager the lower-level role the platform offers, such as admin or content manager, and remove it if you part ways.
- **Put ownership in writing.** The agreement should say the accounts, audiences, and content are yours.
- **Keep the brand assets.** Logos, photos, and templates should be delivered to you in editable form.

## How do you know it's working?

Give it roughly 90 days before judging, and check these in order:

1. **Month one:** a clear plan, a calendar, consistent posting, and content that sounds like your business.
2. **Month two:** you are approving content without friction, and replies to comments and messages are handled.
3. **Month three:** stronger engagement and, above all, more of the thing you set as the goal: messages, calls, bookings, or visits.

If you are not seeing movement, ask for an honest review of what is being posted, not an excuse. A good manager will adjust. ## What is the simplest way to start?

Start small and keep it reversible. Define your goal, shortlist two or three providers, ask the questions above, and look at real work. Begin with a short initial period if the provider allows it, and review the results together.
