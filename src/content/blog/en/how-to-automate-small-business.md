Most small business owners don't have a time problem, they have a repetition problem. The same questions, the same quotes, the same data copied from one place to another. This guide shows you where to start, what you need, and what to avoid.

## What does it mean to automate a small business?

Automating means setting up a system so a repeated task happens without you doing it by hand. Some examples:

- A customer asks for your hours and gets an instant reply.
- A booking form creates a calendar event and sends a reminder.
- A request for a quote fills in a template and lands in your inbox, ready to review.
- A new sale is added to your spreadsheet or accounting tool on its own.

It does **not** mean removing people from your business. It means removing the copy-paste work so people can do what actually needs judgment.

## Which tasks should you automate first?

Write down everything you do in a normal week and circle what you do more than three times. For most small businesses, the best first candidates are:

1. **Repeated customer questions.** Prices, hours, location, how your service works.
2. **Scheduling.** Back-and-forth messages to find a time are expensive in attention.
3. **Quotes and estimates.** If you build them from the same pieces each time, a template or form can do most of the work.
4. **Follow-ups.** Reminders, thank-you messages, payment nudges.
5. **Moving data between tools.** Anything you retype is a candidate.

If you take messages on WhatsApp, we wrote a separate piece on what to automate there and what to leave to a person.

## How do you decide what is worth automating?

Not everything deserves it. Use four simple questions:

- **Is it frequent?** A task you do once a month rarely pays back the effort.
- **Does it follow clear rules?** If you can explain it as if-this-then-that, a machine can do it. If it takes judgment or empathy, keep it human.
- **Is a mistake costly?** Automating something risky without checks can hurt you faster than doing it by hand.
- **Is it stable?** If your process changes every week, fix the process first.

A task that is frequent, rule-based and stable is a green light. A task that is rare and full of exceptions is not.

## Should you use ready-made tools or custom software?

Both are valid. The right choice depends on how complicated your situation is.

**Ready-made tools** work well when:

- The task is simple and common (online booking, email reminders, form notifications).
- You are comfortable adding one more monthly subscription.
- Your needs fit what the tool already does.

**Custom software or tailored automation** works better when:

- You need three or more systems to share information.
- Your pricing, scheduling or approval rules don't fit any standard tool.
- You'd rather own the system than rent it, and avoid piling up monthly fees.

A common path is to start with a simple tool, learn what you really need, and later build something tailored once you know the shape of the process. You can read more about [custom software and automation](/servicios/desarrollo-de-software/?lang=en) if you want to see what that looks like.

## What do you need before you start?

Before you pay for anything, get these ready:

1. **A written process.** Even a rough list of steps: what triggers the task, what happens, who finishes it.
2. **Your common questions and answers.** Collect the 10 to 20 things customers ask most.
3. **A clear owner.** One person decides how the automation works and fixes it when something changes.
4. **A simple measure of success.** Hours saved per week, faster replies, fewer missed appointments. Pick one.
5. **A decent website or contact point.** Automation needs somewhere to start, like a booking form or a contact page. If your site is old or missing, that comes first. See our [web design](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) work if you need one.

## How much does it cost to automate a small business?

Prices vary widely, so treat these as rough market ranges, not quotes:

- **Ready-made tools:** from free plans to roughly $20 to $100 a month per tool, depending on features and volume.
- **Simple custom automation** (for example, a form that creates a quote and notifies you): often from a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars.
- **Larger projects** connecting several systems, with custom rules and dashboards: commonly several thousand dollars and up.

What changes the price most is the number of systems involved, how many exceptions your process has, and whether you need ongoing support. Ask whether there are mandatory monthly fees, because they add up over the years.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Automating a broken process.** You'll just get the same mess, faster.
- **Trying to do everything at once.** Start with one task, measure it, then move on.
- **Hiding the human.** Customers should always be able to reach a person.
- **Choosing tools before defining the problem.** Start with the task, not the software.
- **Not owning your accounts.** Make sure the accounts, data and code are in your name, so you can change providers.
- **Forgetting to test.** Run the automation with real examples, including odd ones, before customers see it.

## What should you ask before hiring someone?

When you talk to a studio or developer, ask:

1. Which exact task will you automate first, and how will we know it works?
2. Will the system and all accounts be in my name?
3. Are there mandatory monthly fees, and what do they cover?
4. What happens when a customer asks something the automation can't handle?
5. How do you handle changes later, and how fast do you respond?
6. Can you work with the tools I already use?

A good provider answers these plainly and starts with your process, not with a catalog of features.
