Taking payments online sounds like a technical project. For most small businesses it isn't. It's a short list of decisions, and you can often be live in a few days. The hard part is choosing the setup that matches how you actually sell.

## What do I need to take payments online?

Four things:

1. **A business bank account.** Keep business money apart from personal money from day one. Taxes, refunds and bookkeeping get much simpler.
2. **A payment processor.** This is the company that charges the customer's card and sends the money to your bank. Many let you open an account for free and charge only per sale.
3. **A place where the customer pays.** A payment link, an invoice, a checkout page on your website, or a booking form that collects payment.
4. **Basic business details.** Your legal business name, tax ID and bank information. Expect a short identity check before you're approved.

You don't need anything custom to start. You need to decide **where** the customer will pay.

## What are the ways to accept online payments?

**Payment links.** You create a link for an amount or a product and send it by text, email or WhatsApp. The customer taps, pays, and you get a receipt. Good for services, deposits and a handful of products. No website required.

**Online invoices.** You bill after the work is done and the client pays from the email. Good for contractors, consultants and anyone selling to other businesses.

**Checkout on your website.** A Buy or Pay button sends the customer to a secure checkout page. Good for selling a few products, classes, packages or deposits without you in the loop.

**A full online store.** Cart, inventory, shipping and taxes. Worth it only if you sell many products. If you sell five things, you don't need a store; you need five buttons.

**Payment inside a booking or quoting flow.** The customer picks a time or accepts a quote and pays a deposit in the same step. This cuts no-shows and back-and-forth messages.

**Bank transfers (ACH).** Usually cheaper for large amounts, but slower. Useful for big invoices.

**Peer-to-peer apps (Zelle, Venmo, Cash App).** Fine for your first sales. The downsides: rules about business use vary, records are scattered, refunds are manual, and customers have less protection, which can make them hesitate.

## How much does it cost to accept online payments?

These are rough market figures. Check each provider's current pricing.

- **Card payments:** typically around 3% plus about 30 cents per transaction. On a $100 sale, that's roughly $3.30.
- **ACH bank payments:** often well under 1%, sometimes capped at a few dollars per transaction.
- **Monthly fees:** some processors charge none; others charge a monthly fee in exchange for lower per-sale rates or extra tools.
- **Disputes (chargebacks):** many processors charge a fee each time a customer disputes a payment, even if you win.
- **The checkout itself:** payment links cost nothing extra. A checkout built into a website depends on the site, and is usually part of the project cost.

Don't choose on the lowest rate alone. Look at how fast you get paid, how disputes are handled, and whether there's real support when something fails.

If you're thinking about adding a surcharge to cover card fees, check the rules first. Card networks and some states restrict it.

## Which option fits my business?

- **You sell services and bill after the job:** online invoices, plus payment links for deposits.
- **You sell appointments or classes:** payment inside a booking flow, so the slot is only held once it's paid or a deposit is in.
- **You sell a few products:** checkout buttons on your website.
- **You sell dozens or hundreds of products:** an online store.
- **You sell to other businesses with large tickets:** invoices with ACH as an option.
- **You're just testing an idea:** payment links, nothing else.

Start with the simplest option that works. You can add more later, and your processor account carries over.

## How do I add payments to my website?

The safest route is a **hosted checkout**: the customer pays on a secure page run by the processor, so card data never passes through your site. It also takes most of the security burden off you.

A site that handles payments well has:

- Secure connection (HTTPS) on every page.
- Clear prices, what's included and a refund policy before the customer pays.
- A short checkout: as few fields and steps as possible.
- A confirmation page and an email receipt.
- A phone-friendly layout, since many customers will pay from their phone.

Test the whole flow yourself before announcing it: pay, get the receipt, request a refund. If you're building or rebuilding the site, [web design](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) is the right moment to include checkout instead of bolting it on later.

If the payment is part of a bigger process, like booking, quoting, or sending data to your spreadsheet or CRM, that's where [custom software and automation](/servicios/desarrollo-de-software/?lang=en) saves you hours of manual follow-up.

## What about taxes, refunds and disputes?

- **Sales tax:** whether you must collect it depends on your state and what you sell. Ask your accountant before you launch, not after.
- **Refunds:** write a simple policy and show it at checkout. It prevents most disputes.
- **Disputes:** keep the order, the delivery or service proof, and your messages with the customer. You'll need them if a payment is challenged.
- **Records:** your processor should let you export a sales report. Your accountant will ask for it.

## What mistakes should I avoid?

- **Using a personal account** for business payments.
- **Collecting card numbers** by email, DM or phone notes. Use a payment link instead.
- **No receipt or confirmation.** Customers think the payment failed and pay twice, or call you.
- **A long checkout.** Every extra field loses some buyers.
- **Never testing on a phone.**
- **Letting someone else own the account.** If your processor account or website is in a freelancer's name, you're stuck if they disappear.

## What should I ask the person setting it up?

1. Which processor will you use, and why? Will the account be in my name?
2. Is the checkout hosted by the processor?
3. What are the total costs: setup, monthly, and per sale?
4. What happens when a payment fails or I need to refund?
5. Can I export my sales records?
6. Can this connect to my booking, invoicing or email?
7. Who can I contact if checkout stops working?

Vague answers to these are a warning sign.
