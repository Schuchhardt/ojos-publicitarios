Most small business websites fail for a simple reason: they are built around what the owner wants to say, not around what a stranger needs in order to decide. This checklist follows the order in which a visitor goes looking for things.

## What should a visitor understand in the first five seconds?

Three things: **what you do, who it's for, and where you work.** If the top of your homepage doesn't answer those, nothing below it will save you.

Compare "Welcome to our website, where quality meets passion" with "Family-run plumbing in Austin. Same-day repairs for homes." The second one tells a stranger whether they are in the right place.

At the top of the page, put:

- A headline that states the service and the audience.
- One sentence of detail (what makes you a safe choice).
- **One main button**: call, book, or request a quote.
- A real photo of your work, your team or your location.

## What pages does a small business website need?

For most businesses, this is enough to start:

- **Home:** the summary and the main action.
- **Services or products:** what you offer, in plain language. If you have several distinct services, give each its own page. It is easier to read and easier for Google to match to a search.
- **About:** who is behind the business, how long you've done this, where you operate.
- **Contact:** every way to reach you, in one place.

Useful additions once the basics are solid: a page with reviews or past work, and an FAQ that answers the questions you get every week. If you are still deciding how big the site should be, our [web design service](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) starts from exactly that question.

## What information must be easy to find?

People arrive with practical questions. Make the answers impossible to miss:

- Business name, phone, email and address or **service area**.
- Opening hours and how fast you reply.
- What you offer and what you don't.
- **Pricing guidance:** exact prices, "starting at" figures, typical ranges, or an explanation of how quotes work.
- How the process works: what happens after someone contacts you.
- How to pay, book or order.

On mobile, the phone number should be tappable. And keep your name, address and phone identical on your site and your Google Business Profile. Inconsistencies confuse both customers and search engines.

## How should people contact you or book?

Choose **one primary action** and make it obvious on every page. The usual options:

- A phone call (best for urgent services).
- A short contact or quote-request form.
- An online booking calendar.
- A chat or messaging button.

Keep forms short. Name, a way to reach them and what they need is usually plenty. Every extra field loses some people.

Also decide what happens next. Does the visitor get a confirmation? Who replies, and how fast? ## What builds trust with a stranger?

A new visitor doesn't know you. Give them reasons to believe you:

- **Real photos** of you, your team and your work. Stock photos alone feel empty.
- **Reviews and testimonials** from real customers, with their permission. Never invent them.
- Licenses, insurance, certifications or memberships, if they apply to your trade.
- Clear policies: warranties, refunds, cancellations.
- A face and a name on the About page.

If you don't have reviews yet, show examples of finished work and explain your process instead.

## What technical basics can't be skipped?

These are invisible when they work and costly when they don't:

- **Mobile-friendly design.** A large share of your visitors will be on phones. Test your own site on yours.
- **Speed.** Heavy images and bloated templates make people leave.
- **HTTPS** (the padlock). Browsers warn visitors away from sites without it.
- **Basic SEO:** a clear page title and description, proper headings, the city or area you serve written in the text, and a link to your Google Business Profile.
- **Analytics**, so you know which pages and buttons are used.
- **Accessibility basics:** readable contrast, image descriptions, labeled form fields. Accessibility is also a legal topic in the US, so ask your builder how they handle it.
- **Ownership.** The domain and hosting should be in your name, with you holding the logins.

## What can you leave out at the start?

A lean site that is clear beats a big site that is confusing. You can skip, at least at launch:

- A blog with no plan to feed it.
- A chatbot nobody maintains.
- Auto-playing videos, rotating sliders and pop-ups.
- Social media feeds that slow the page down.
- Fifteen pages where five would do.

You can always add later, once you see what visitors actually use.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- Talking about "we" instead of what the customer gets.
- Hiding the contact details or burying the button.
- Outdated info: old hours, closed locations, dead links.
- No prices and no hint of how to get one.
- Several competing buttons instead of one clear next step.
- Building on a platform or with a provider that keeps your domain and content hostage.

## What should you ask whoever builds your site?

Before you sign anything, ask:

1. Who will own the domain, hosting accounts and the site itself?
2. Are there mandatory monthly fees, and for what exactly?
3. How will it look and perform on a phone?
4. What SEO basics are included?
5. How will contact forms or bookings reach me, and can they connect to my calendar or tools?
6. Who updates the site after launch, and how long does a change take?
7. Can I see the full scope in writing, with what is and isn't included?

Vague answers to these are a warning sign.
