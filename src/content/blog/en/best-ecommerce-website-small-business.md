Most owners who search for the best ecommerce website are not really asking for a brand name. They are asking: **how do I start selling online without wasting money or locking myself into something I will regret?** This guide helps you decide what type of store you need, what it should include and what to ask before you hire anyone.

## What does "best ecommerce website" actually mean for a small business?

The best option is not the one with the most features. It is the one that:

- Lets you **add and change products yourself**, without calling a developer for every price change.
- Handles **payments, shipping and taxes** the way your business needs.
- Does not eat your margin with fees you did not see coming.
- Can **grow with you**, or at least lets you leave with your data if you outgrow it.
- Belongs to you: the domain, the content and the customer list.

Start there, then look at the options.

## What are your options: marketplace, store builder or custom store?

There are three realistic paths for a small business.

### Marketplace

You list products on a large platform that already has buyers. Setup is fast and traffic is built in.

**Good for:** testing products, clearing inventory, getting your first sales.

**Watch out for:** fees on every sale, price competition, rules you do not control and the fact that **the customer belongs to the platform**, not to you.

### Hosted store builder

You pay a monthly plan and use ready-made templates to build a store. Hosting, checkout and security are included.

**Good for:** most small businesses selling physical or digital products at fixed prices.

**Watch out for:** monthly fees that grow as you add apps, transaction fees, and design limits. If the store looks like everyone else's, it will not stand out.

### Custom store

A store designed and built around your business, either from scratch or on top of an open platform.

**Good for:** businesses with special processes, strong branding needs, or integrations with other systems (inventory, invoicing, WhatsApp, scheduling).

**Watch out for:** higher upfront cost and a longer timeline. It only pays off if the customization solves a real problem.

## How do you know which one fits your business?

Answer these questions honestly:

1. **How many products do you sell?** Under a few hundred with simple variations: a builder works. Thousands, or complex options: look at custom.
2. **Are prices fixed?** If you need quotes, custom orders or different prices per client, a standard store will fight you.
3. **Do you ship physical goods?** Then shipping rates, labels and returns matter more than design.
4. **Do you already have customers?** If you do, your own store is a better long-term asset. If you have none, a marketplace can help you learn what sells.
5. **How much time can you spend managing it?** A store is not a one-time project. Orders, stock and messages need attention.
6. **Do you want to avoid ongoing platform fees?** Builders charge monthly. A custom store can be set up in your name without mandatory subscriptions, though third-party costs like payment processing still apply.

If you answered "simple products, fixed prices, no special process," start with a builder. If two or three answers point to complexity, get a custom quote before committing.

## What should any online store include?

No matter which route you pick, check this list:

- **Clear product pages** with real photos, price, shipping info and a direct call to buy.
- **Simple checkout** with few steps, guest purchase and the payment methods your customers actually use.
- **Mobile-first design.** Most shoppers will see your store on a phone first.
- **Shipping and returns policy** visible before checkout, not buried in the footer.
- **Trust signals:** contact details, real reviews if you have them, secure payment, a physical or business address.
- **Taxes configured correctly** for the states where you must collect sales tax. Ask an accountant if you are unsure.
- **Basic SEO:** descriptive product titles, readable URLs and fast loading, so people can find you on Google.
- **Accessibility basics,** such as alt text on images and readable contrast. It helps customers and reduces legal risk.
- **Email capture,** so you can contact customers again without paying for ads every time.
- **A way to answer questions fast,** like a contact form or WhatsApp link. Unanswered questions are lost sales.

## How much does an ecommerce website cost?

Prices vary a lot. As **rough market ranges, not quotes**:

- **Marketplace:** little or no setup cost, but a percentage of each sale plus listing or fulfillment fees.
- **Store builder, doing it yourself:** about $30 to $100 a month for the plan, plus payment processing (commonly around 3% plus a small fixed fee per transaction) and any paid apps.
- **Store builder, set up by a professional:** often a few thousand dollars for design, product loading and configuration.
- **Custom store:** commonly from around $5,000 and up to $30,000 or more, depending on features and integrations.

Remember the costs that are easy to forget: the domain, product photography, shipping materials, apps or plugins, and the time you spend running the store.

The cheapest option is not always cheaper. A builder with five paid add-ons can cost more per year than a well-built custom store, and a custom store you do not need is money wasted.

## What mistakes do small businesses make with online stores?

- **Choosing by price alone** and discovering later that the platform charges for the things you need.
- **Building on a marketplace only,** so they have sales but no customer list.
- **Launching with every product** instead of starting with the best sellers and doing them well.
- **Poor photos and thin descriptions.** A shopper cannot touch the product, so the page has to do the work.
- **Hidden shipping costs** that appear at the last step and cause abandoned carts.
- **Ignoring how orders get handled.** If every order means copying data by hand into a spreadsheet, the store will create more work than it saves. This is where [custom software and automation](/servicios/desarrollo-de-software/?lang=en) can connect orders, inventory and customer messages.
- **Not owning the account.** Make sure the domain, store and payment accounts are in your name.

## What should you ask whoever builds your store?

If you hire someone, ask:

1. **Who owns the domain, the store and the payment accounts?** The answer should be you.
2. **What are the ongoing costs** after launch, including platform, hosting and apps?
3. **Can I edit products, prices and content myself?** And will you show me how?
4. **How will orders, stock and shipping be handled?** Is anything automated?
5. **What happens if I want to leave?** Can I export my products and customers?
6. **How will the store be found?** Ask about SEO basics and how product pages are structured.
7. **What is included in the price,** and what counts as extra?
8. **How fast do you respond** when something breaks during a sale?

Vague answers are a warning sign. A good partner explains trade-offs in plain language and does not push the most expensive option by default.
