If your business still runs on word of mouth, a Facebook page or a website from 2015, getting online can feel like a pile of decisions: builder or developer, which social networks, what to pay for. The good news is that **the order matters more than the budget**. Here is a plan you can follow without being technical.

## What does 'getting your business online' actually mean?

It means a stranger who hears your name, or searches for what you sell, can find you, understand what you do in under a minute, and contact you or buy. That is the whole goal. Not every business needs an app, a blog and five social profiles.

In practice it breaks into five pieces:

1. **Your name:** a domain and a business email.
2. **A website** that explains what you offer and how to reach you.
3. **A Google presence**, mainly a Google Business Profile.
4. **One or two social profiles** you can keep up.
5. **A way to handle repetitive work:** messages, bookings, quotes.

## How do you claim your name online?

Before building anything, secure your names. Buy a domain (yourbusiness.com), set up an email on it (hello@yourbusiness.com instead of a Gmail address), and reserve the same handle on the social networks you might use.

One rule matters more than the rest: **everything must be registered in your name and with your email.** Domain, hosting, social accounts, Google profile. It is common for owners to find out years later that a former designer or a relative owns the domain, and they cannot change anything. Ask for admin access to everything and keep it.

A domain usually costs around $10 to $20 a year, and business email is typically a few dollars per user per month. Those are approximate figures.

## Do you need a website if you already have social media?

In most cases, yes. Social profiles are rented space: the platform controls who sees your posts, can change the rules, and can lock your account. A website is the one place you own, where you control the message, and the one Google can show when someone searches.

It also answers a basic trust question. Many customers look for a website before they call or buy. A simple, clear one beats none.

## What kind of website should you start with?

It depends on what you do:

- **Landing page:** one page, one goal (call, book, request a quote). Good for a single service or a fast start.
- **Business website:** several pages (home, services, about, contact). Right for most service businesses.
- **Online store:** if you sell products and want to take payments online.

Start with the smallest option that does the job, and make sure it can grow. A solid one-page site that brings calls is better than a six-page site that never gets finished. If you want help choosing, see our [web design service](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en).

Whatever you choose, every site needs:

- What you do and where, visible on the first screen.
- Your services, explained in plain words.
- A clear way to contact you: phone, form or messaging.
- Real photos of your work or your team.
- Fast loading and a layout that works on a phone.
- Basics like hours, address and service area.

## How do you get found on Google?

Three things, in this order:

1. **Google Business Profile.** It is free. Claim it, verify it, and fill in category, hours, photos and services. For local businesses it is often the biggest early win.
2. **Clear pages on your website** that use the words customers use. 'Emergency plumber in Tampa' works; 'premium hydraulic solutions' does not.
3. **Reviews.** Ask happy customers to leave one, and reply to every review.

SEO takes months. Be wary of anyone who guarantees the first page in a few weeks.

## Which social networks should you use?

Pick **one or two where your customers already are**, not all of them. A restaurant or salon usually does well on Instagram and Facebook. A business that sells to other businesses often belongs on LinkedIn.

Choose a rhythm you can sustain. A few good posts a week beat daily posting for two weeks followed by silence. If you do not have the time, handing it off is reasonable, as long as the accounts stay in your name. ## What should you automate from the start?

You do not need a big system on day one. But repetitive tasks eat hours: answering the same questions, scheduling, sending quotes.

Once your site is live, watch what you answer ten times a week. Common first steps are:

- A contact form that sends requests to your email and a spreadsheet.
- A booking link instead of back-and-forth messages.
- An automatic reply that confirms you received a request.
- Pre-written answers to the most frequent questions.

Automate after you see the real questions, not before. When simple tools stop being enough, [custom software and automation](/servicios/desarrollo-de-software/?lang=en) can connect your site, your messages and your records.

## How much does it cost to get a business online?

These are broad, approximate market ranges in the US. They vary a lot by provider and scope:

- **Domain:** about $10 to $20 a year.
- **Business email:** a few dollars per mailbox per month.
- **DIY website builder:** roughly $15 to $50 a month, plus your own time.
- **Professionally built small business website:** from around $1,500 to $10,000 or more, depending on pages, design and custom features.
- **Online store:** usually more than a standard site.
- **Social media management:** often several hundred to a few thousand dollars per month.
- **Google Business Profile:** free.

Watch the fine print. Low monthly fees add up over the years, and some providers keep your site on their platform so you cannot take it with you. Ask who owns what.

## How long does it take?

With your content ready, a realistic plan looks like this:

- **Week 1:** domain, email, Google Business Profile, social handles.
- **Week 2:** write the texts and gather photos.
- **Week 3:** build, review and launch the site; set up your main social profile.
- **Week 4:** see what customers ask and automate the first repetitive task.

Appearing well in search results takes longer, and it improves as you add reviews and useful content.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Starting on five social networks** and keeping none up to date.
- **Paying for a site you do not own** or cannot move.
- **Building without a goal.** Decide what a visitor should do: call, book, buy.
- **Vague copy and generic photos** that could belong to any company.
- **Ignoring mobile.** Most visitors will see your site on a phone.
- **Hiding your contact info** instead of putting it up front.
- **Never updating anything.** Old hours or prices cost you customers.

## What should you ask before hiring someone?

- Who will own the domain, the site and the accounts? Will I get admin access?
- Are there mandatory monthly fees to keep the site online?
- What exactly is included: pages, revisions, texts, photos, timeline?
- Can I edit simple things myself?
- Will it be fast and work well on phones?
- Can you show similar work or put me in touch with past clients?
- How quickly do you answer when something breaks?

If someone cannot answer these plainly, keep looking.
