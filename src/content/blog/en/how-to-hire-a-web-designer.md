Hiring a web designer feels risky because most owners can't judge the work until it's finished. You can lower that risk a lot with a clear brief, a handful of good questions and a contract that says who owns what. Here is how to do it step by step.

## What kind of web designer do you actually need?

There are four common options, and they suit different situations:

- **Freelancer.** One person, usually the lowest price and the most flexibility. The risk: if they get busy or disappear, you're stuck. Good for simple sites.
- **Small studio.** A small team that covers design, development and often copy and SEO. For most small businesses this is the best balance of price, quality and continuity.
- **Large agency.** Heavy process and high prices. Usually more than a small business needs.
- **Website builder plus a helper.** Platforms like Wix, Squarespace or Shopify, with someone to set them up. Fine for simple needs, but you stay limited by the platform.

It also helps to know the difference between a **designer** and a **developer**. The designer decides how the site looks and how visitors move through it. The developer builds it and handles forms, bookings, payments, speed and integrations. For a basic brochure site, one person can do both. For an online store or anything connected to other tools, make sure someone on the team has real development experience.

## What should you prepare before contacting anyone?

Most bad quotes come from vague requests. Before you write to anyone, put together:

1. **The goal.** Get calls? Sell products? Book appointments? Look credible to new customers? Pick one main goal.
2. **The pages you think you need.** Home, services, about, contact. Rough is fine.
3. **Content.** Logo, brand colors, photos and the basic text. Ask whether the designer helps with writing, because many don't.
4. **Examples.** Two or three sites you like and one you dislike, with a sentence on why.
5. **A budget range and a date.** Even a rough range saves everyone time.
6. **What the site must connect to.** Email, a calendar, payments, a CRM, WhatsApp or a spreadsheet.

If you're unsure what type of site fits, read our guide on [web design for small businesses](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) to see how landing pages, company sites and online stores differ.

## Where do you find a good web designer?

Location matters less than it used to. Plenty of good designers and studios work fully remotely, and that's fine if they communicate well. Places to look:

- **Referrals** from other business owners. This is the strongest signal, because you can ask how the project actually went.
- **Sites you admire.** Many sites credit their designer in the footer, or you can simply ask the owner.
- **Portfolios and directories.** Useful for building a shortlist, but treat them as a starting point.
- **Search results.** Look for studios whose own website is clear, fast and easy to understand. If they can't present themselves well, be careful.

Aim for a shortlist of **three to five** candidates. More than that and you'll struggle to compare.

## How do you tell a good designer from a risky one?

Look at evidence, not promises.

**Good signs**
- Portfolio with **live sites** you can open on your phone, not only polished images.
- They ask about your customers, goals and business before talking about colors.
- A clear process: discovery, design, review, build, launch.
- They explain things in plain language and answer on time.
- A written quote that lists exactly what's included.

**Warning signs**
- A price with no scope behind it.
- Guarantees like first page on Google in a few weeks.
- Pressure to sign today.
- They keep the domain, hosting or files under their own account.
- Portfolio sites that are broken, slow or look unchanged from a template.
- Vague answers about who will actually do the work.

Open two or three of their live projects on your phone. Check how fast they load and whether the text is readable. It takes five minutes and tells you a lot.

## What should you ask before hiring?

Use the same questions with every candidate so the answers are comparable:

1. Who will work on my project, and will I talk to them directly?
2. What exactly is included: number of pages, revisions, mobile version, forms, basic SEO setup?
3. What do you need from me, and how long does it usually take?
4. How long will the project take, and what slows it down?
5. Will the site be built on a platform or custom? What are the limits of each?
6. Who owns the domain, hosting and site files once we're done?
7. Are there monthly fees I'm required to pay to keep the site running?
8. What happens after launch? Is there support, and for how long?
9. Can I update text and photos myself?
10. Can I talk to a past client?

Question 7 matters more than it seems. Some providers build a site cheaply and then charge a monthly fee to keep it hosted on their system, which makes it hard to leave. Ask for the full ongoing cost, including domain and hosting, in writing.

## How much does it cost to hire a web designer?

Prices vary widely, so treat these as **rough market ranges** in the US, not quotes:

- **Simple site on a template or builder, set up by a freelancer:** roughly $500 to $3,000.
- **Custom small business site (5 to 10 pages) from a freelancer or small studio:** roughly $3,000 to $10,000.
- **Larger custom site or online store with many products and integrations:** roughly $10,000 to $30,000 or more.
- **Ongoing costs:** domain and hosting commonly run from tens to a few hundred dollars per year, plus any tools or maintenance you add.

What moves the price: number of pages, custom design versus template, online sales, integrations, who writes the content and how many revision rounds you expect. For a deeper breakdown, see our note on [how much a small business website should cost](/blog/). 

When you compare quotes, compare the **scope**, not the total. A cheaper quote that excludes mobile design, content or revisions often ends up costing more.

## What should the contract say?

Even for a small project, get it in writing. At minimum:

- **Scope:** pages, features and deliverables.
- **Price and payment schedule:** commonly a deposit, a middle payment and a final payment after launch.
- **Timeline** and what each side must deliver and by when.
- **Revisions:** how many rounds are included and what extra ones cost.
- **Ownership:** the domain, hosting account and site files in **your name**, with you holding the logins.
- **Content rights:** you own your text, photos and logo, and the designer confirms any images or fonts they supply are properly licensed.
- **After launch:** support period, maintenance options and cost.
- **Exit terms:** what happens if either side wants to stop.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Choosing only on price.** The cheapest option often leaves out content, mobile design or support.
- **Starting without a goal.** A site with no purpose rarely produces results.
- **Not preparing content.** Delays usually come from the owner's side, because text and photos arrive late.
- **Skipping the ownership question.** Fixing this later can be hard and expensive.
- **Expecting leads right after launch.** A new site needs traffic from search, social media, ads or referrals. If you also need that, look at [digital marketing and social media](/servicios/marketing-digital/?lang=en) separately.
- **Ignoring what happens after launch.** Sites need updates, backups and small fixes.
- **Too many decision-makers.** Pick one person to give feedback so revisions don't spiral.
