If you have decided to hand your social media to someone else, the next problem is that every proposal looks different. One offers 12 posts, another offers a strategy, another offers growth. This guide explains what these services actually include, so you can compare them on equal terms.

## What does a social media management service actually include?

Most services are built from the same pieces. The difference is how many of them you get and how well they are done:

- **A content plan.** A monthly calendar of topics tied to what your business sells, not generic holiday posts.
- **Content creation.** Designed posts, captions and, in some packages, short videos or Reels.
- **Publishing.** Posts scheduled and published on the platforms you agreed on.
- **Community management.** Replying to comments and direct messages, or at least flagging the ones that need you.
- **Reporting.** A monthly summary of what was published and what happened.

Some providers also offer paid ads, photo or video production, and profile setup or cleanup. Those are usually separate line items, and that is fine, as long as it is clear up front.

## Is it the same as hiring a social media manager?

It overlaps, but the question is different. Hiring a person is about who you trust. Choosing a service is about **what you get each month**. A good service has a defined process: how content is proposed, how you approve it, and how quickly things get published. If you are still deciding between an individual and a team, our own work in [digital marketing and social media](/servicios/marketing-digital/?lang=en) is built around that kind of monthly routine, but the checklist below works for anyone you evaluate.

## What should the monthly deliverables look like?

Ask for numbers and formats, in writing. A vague promise like active management of your networks is not a deliverable. A useful list looks like this:

1. Number of posts per month, per platform.
2. Formats: single images, carousels, short videos.
3. How many rounds of changes you get on each piece.
4. How fast comments and messages are answered, and during what hours.
5. What the monthly report contains.
6. What is **not** included, such as ads budget, photography or new accounts.

More posts is not automatically better. For a local business, three or four good posts a week that show real products, people and results will beat daily filler.

## What does the provider need from you?

This is where many projects stall. Whoever manages your social media cannot invent your business. Expect to provide:

- Photos of your work, products, team and location, or a plan to get them.
- Your prices, offers, hours and the questions customers ask most.
- Fast approvals. If drafts sit for two weeks, the calendar falls apart.
- Access to your accounts as an admin or collaborator, never your personal password.

If a provider asks nothing about your customers and just sends a price, that tells you how generic the content will be.

## Who owns the accounts and the content?

This point matters more than any feature. **The accounts should be in your name**, created with your email or your business profile, and the provider should work as a manager or collaborator. You should also own the content created for you: images, captions and videos.

It is a bad sign if the provider wants to create the accounts under their own login, or if leaving means you lose your followers. Put it in the agreement: if the relationship ends, you keep the accounts and access is removed.

## How do you compare proposals?

Line up two or three proposals and compare the same things:

- **Scope:** posts, formats, platforms, replies.
- **Process:** who writes, who designs, how approvals work.
- **Contract terms:** minimum commitment, notice to cancel, what happens to the accounts.
- **Reporting:** what you will see each month and what decisions it supports.
- **Price:** only after the above is equal.

Market prices vary a lot. As a rough, approximate range, light posting on one or two platforms can run from a few hundred dollars a month, while fuller programs with video and community replies can reach a few thousand. A lower price usually means fewer posts, less custom work or less time spent on your business.

## What are the red flags?

- **Guaranteed followers or viral results.** Nobody controls that, and bought followers do not buy customers.
- **No questions about your business.** Generic templates are easy to spot.
- **Long contracts with no exit.** Especially with penalties for leaving.
- **Refusing to share access or reports.** You should see what is being done in your name.
- **Reporting only vanity numbers.** Likes alone say little about whether the work helps you sell.

## How do you know the service is working?

Decide on a few signs before you start, and review them monthly. For most small businesses these are more useful than follower counts:

- Messages and inquiries that mention seeing you on social media.
- Profile visits and clicks to your website or WhatsApp.
- Engagement from real local customers, not random accounts.
- Consistency: did everything promised actually get published?

Give it about three months before judging. If after that you see good output but no movement, the issue might be the offer, the website it points to, or the lack of ads, not only the posts.

## Do you need paid ads on top of it?

Not always. Organic posting builds trust and gives people something to see when they look you up. Ads speed up reach, especially for a new business or a specific promotion. If you do add ads, treat the budget as separate from the management fee and ask who controls the ad account. Same rule: it should be yours.

Also check where social media sends people. If the link in your bio leads to an outdated site, the posts are doing half the job. A clear page behind the profile, built with [web design](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) that fits your business, makes every post work harder.
