Your online presence is what a stranger finds when they type your business name, or the service you offer, into a search box. It is not one thing. It is a handful of places that together answer three questions: **Are you real? Do you do what I need? How do I reach you?**

If those answers are easy to find and consistent, you look trustworthy. If they are missing or contradict each other, people move on to someone else.

## What does an online presence actually include?

For a small business, five pieces cover almost everything:

- **A Google Business Profile:** the listing that appears on Google Search and Maps with your hours, address or service area, phone number, photos and reviews.
- **A website you own:** your home base, with your services, proof that you do good work and a clear way to contact you.
- **One or two social media accounts:** where customers check that you are active and see your work.
- **Reviews:** what other customers say about you, mostly on Google.
- **Consistent basic information:** the same name, phone, address and hours everywhere your business appears.

You do not need ten platforms, a podcast or a daily video routine. You need these five, done properly.

## What should you set up first?

In this order:

1. **Google Business Profile.** It is free and fast, and for a local business it is often the first thing a customer sees.
2. **Website.** It takes longer, but it is the only piece you fully control.
3. **One social channel.** Choose it, then keep it alive.
4. **A habit for collecting reviews.**

The order matters because effort and payoff are uneven. A profile takes an afternoon. A website takes weeks. Social media never really ends. Start with what is cheap and visible.

## How do you set up a Google Business Profile?

Create the profile with the exact business name you use in real life, choose the most accurate category, and add your hours, phone number and website. If you travel to customers, list your service area instead of showing a street address.

Then finish it. Add real photos of your work, your team or your storefront, a short description in plain language, and your main services. Google will ask you to verify that the business is yours, so use a real address or phone number you control.

The common mistake is creating it and forgetting it. Hours that are wrong during a holiday or a phone number that no longer works cost you customers directly.

## Do you really need a website if you have social media?

Yes, in most cases. Social accounts are rented space: the platform can change its rules, hide your posts or suspend your account, and you lose your audience with it. A website is yours, and it is where someone who found you on Google or Instagram goes to decide.

A small business site does not have to be big. It needs a clear explanation of what you do, who it is for, where you work, proof (photos, results, reviews), and one obvious way to contact you. If you want the details, our guide to [web design](/servicios/diseno-web/?lang=en) shows what a site like that involves.

Two things to insist on whoever builds it: the domain and the site should be registered in your name, and it should work well on a phone, since that is where many of your customers will see it first.

## Which social media channels should you choose?

Do not choose based on what is trendy. Choose based on where your customers already are and what you can keep up.

- **Facebook and Instagram:** useful for local services, restaurants, beauty, retail and anything visual.
- **LinkedIn:** useful if you sell to other businesses or professionals.
- **TikTok or YouTube:** useful if you can produce short videos consistently and your product benefits from being shown.

One active account beats five abandoned ones. A profile whose last post was eighteen months ago can make a business look closed.

What to post is simpler than it seems: your work, before-and-after results, answers to questions customers ask, and the people behind the business. ## How do you get customers to leave reviews?

Ask. Most satisfied customers never think to write a review unless someone makes it easy.

- Ask right after a job goes well, while the customer is happy.
- Send a direct link to your Google review page by text or email.
- Make it one request, not three reminders.
- Reply to every review, including the bad ones, politely and briefly.

Do not buy reviews or write them yourself. Platforms can remove them, and customers can usually tell.

## Why does consistency matter so much?

If your website says you open at 9, your Google listing says 10 and your Facebook page has an old phone number, customers get confused and search engines get less certain about which information is right.

Create a simple one-page document with your exact business name, address or service area, phone, email, hours and a short description. Use it every time you create or update a listing. Review it whenever something changes.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Starting everywhere at once** and maintaining nothing.
- **Having no website** and relying only on a social account.
- **Using a free site address** with someone else's name in it, which looks temporary.
- **Leaving old information online** after you move, change your number or change prices.
- **Ignoring messages and reviews.** An unanswered message on your page is a lost customer.
- **Paying for a package you do not understand,** especially one that promises to put you in first place on Google.

## How much does it cost?

The ranges below are rough market figures in the US, not quotes, and they vary widely by provider and scope:

- **Google Business Profile:** free to create. You pay only if someone sets it up for you.
- **Website:** from a few hundred dollars for a very basic template to several thousand for a custom small business site.
- **Social media management:** often from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars per month, depending on how many channels and how much content.
- **Your own time:** the cost people forget. Posting, answering messages and asking for reviews takes hours every week.

What matters is what is included and who owns it afterward. Ask that the domain, the website and the social accounts are in your name.

## Should you do it yourself or hire someone?

A reasonable split for many owners: do the Google profile, the contact details and the review requests yourself, because they take little time and depend on knowing your own business. Hire help for the website and for consistent content, because those take skill and hours you may need for the work that earns money.

Before hiring, ask:

1. What exactly will I receive, and in what time frame?
2. Will the website, domain and accounts be in my name?
3. Are there monthly fees I cannot cancel?
4. Who updates the site and posts when something changes?
5. Can I see similar work and talk to someone about their experience?

A good provider answers these directly. Be cautious about anyone who avoids them.
