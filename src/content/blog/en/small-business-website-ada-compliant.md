You got a scary email, heard about a lawsuit from another owner, or your web designer mentioned "accessibility." Now you want a straight answer: does your website have to be ADA compliant?

Here is the honest version. The law is not perfectly clear, the risk is real, and the fix is mostly good web design. (This is general information, not legal advice. For your specific case, talk to a lawyer.)

## So, do small business websites have to be ADA compliant?

**Treat it as yes.** The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) was written in 1990, before business websites existed. Title III requires businesses open to the public not to discriminate against people with disabilities. It doesn't contain a technical checklist for websites.

Courts haven't been fully consistent about when a website counts. Some tie it to a physical location, others look at whether the site is a way of reaching customers. Meanwhile, businesses of all sizes have received demand letters and lawsuits over inaccessible sites.

Because the rules are fuzzy and the cost of ignoring them can be high, the practical answer is: **build your site to be accessible and don't bet on a gray area.**

## What does "ADA compliant" actually mean for a website?

There is no official ADA seal. What people mean in practice is meeting the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)**, usually **version 2.1, Level AA**.

WCAG is organized around four ideas: content should be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. In plain language:

- People who can't see the screen can still understand it (screen readers).
- People who can't use a mouse can still navigate (keyboard only).
- Text is readable, with enough contrast and resizable.
- Forms, buttons, and errors are clear.

In 2024 the Department of Justice finalized a rule that adopts WCAG 2.1 AA for state and local government websites. It doesn't apply to private businesses, but it shows which benchmark is the reference.

## Is my business really at risk?

The risk is higher if you:

- **Sell online** or take bookings and payments through your site.
- Serve the public, such as restaurants, clinics, salons, stores, and hotels.
- Have a site with obvious barriers: no image descriptions, tiny gray text, forms that can't be completed with a keyboard.

The risk is lower, but not zero, for a simple brochure site. Some states also have laws that make claims easier to bring. Many cases are settled with fixes and fees, which is expensive and stressful for a small business even if you did nothing malicious.

The silver lining: accessibility also helps real customers. People with low vision, color blindness, hearing loss, motor limitations, or just a phone in bright sunlight all benefit.

## What are the most common problems on small business sites?

Most issues are repetitive. If you fix these, you've handled a big share of the typical complaints:

1. **Images without alt text.** Screen readers need a short description of meaningful images.
2. **Low color contrast.** Light gray text on white, or text over a photo, is hard to read.
3. **Forms without labels.** Placeholder text alone isn't enough. Each field needs a real label.
4. **No keyboard navigation.** You should be able to reach menus, buttons, and forms using the Tab key, with a visible focus outline.
5. **Vague links.** "Click here" says nothing. "View our menu" does.
6. **Messy headings.** Headings should follow a logical order, not be used just to make text big.
7. **Videos without captions** and audio without transcripts.
8. **Pop-ups that trap focus** or can't be closed without a mouse.
9. **Inaccessible PDFs**, such as menus or price lists saved as scanned images.

## How do I check my own site?

You can do a first pass in an afternoon, for free:

- **Run an automated scan.** Free tools like WAVE or the accessibility report in Chrome's Lighthouse catch the obvious issues. They only detect a portion of problems, so a clean score doesn't mean you're done.
- **Try the keyboard test.** Put your mouse aside and press Tab through your homepage. Can you reach everything? Can you see where you are? Can you submit a form?
- **Zoom to 200%.** Does the layout still work?
- **Read your pages with a screen reader** for a few minutes, or ask someone who uses one.
- **Check your contact and checkout paths first.** Those are the most important and the most likely to be challenged.

## Do accessibility widgets and overlays fix it?

You have probably seen ads for a plugin that makes your site "ADA compliant in 5 minutes." Be careful.

Overlays add a toolbar to adjust font size or contrast. Some of those features are useful. But they don't repair broken structure, missing labels, or poor keyboard support, and many accessibility experts and users are critical of them. Sites using overlays have also been named in complaints.

Use a widget as an extra, if you want, **never as your plan.** The solid approach is to fix the underlying site.

## What does it cost to make a website accessible?

Prices vary widely, so treat these as rough market ranges:

- **Building accessibility into a new site:** usually a modest part of the overall project, because it's much easier to do correctly from the start.
- **Fixing an existing small site:** often from a few hundred dollars to a few thousand, depending on size, platform, and how many problems there are.
- **Professional manual audit:** frequently from a few hundred to several thousand dollars, more for large or ecommerce sites.
- **Legal costs** from a demand letter or lawsuit can quickly exceed all of the above.

If your site is old and heavy with problems, a rebuild can cost less than patching.

## What should I ask a designer or developer?

Before hiring, ask:

- **Do you build to WCAG 2.1 AA?** A clear yes, with examples of how, is what you want.
- **How do you test?** Look for both automated tools and manual checks (keyboard, screen reader).
- **Will the templates and plugins you use be accessible?** Themes can be the source of many problems.
- **How will I keep it accessible when I add content?** Ask for a short guide on alt text, headings, and captions.
- **Will you fix issues if something is found later?** Get this in writing.

Be wary of anyone who promises "100% guaranteed ADA compliance." Nobody can promise legal immunity. A good provider promises a solid standard and honest testing.

## What if I get a demand letter?

Don't ignore it and don't pay immediately. Keep the letter, don't change evidence on the site yet, and **talk to a lawyer** who handles ADA matters. Then get an accessibility review so the underlying problems are fixed. Fixing the site is useful both for your customers and for any response.
