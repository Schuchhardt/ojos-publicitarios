Your website doesn't stop costing money the day it launches. But the ongoing bill is usually much smaller than people fear, as long as you know which lines are real and which are upsells.

## How much does it cost to maintain a small business website?

For most small businesses, **roughly $20 to $60 a month** covers the basics (domain, hosting and business email) if you or your team handle small text and photo changes yourselves. If someone else handles updates, backups, security and changes, plan on **roughly $100 to $400 a month all in**.

These are broad, approximate market ranges, not quotes. Your number depends on the type of site, how much traffic it gets, and how often it changes.

## What costs can't you avoid?

Every site has a few fixed costs:

- **Domain name:** roughly $10 to $25 a year for a standard .com. Premium names that someone already owns can cost far more.
- **Hosting:** the server where your site lives. Shared hosting for a simple site is often $5 to $30 a month. Better or managed hosting for a store or a busier site can be $25 to $100 or more.
- **SSL certificate:** the padlock that makes the site secure. It's included free with most hosting today, so be suspicious if someone bills it separately at a high price.
- **Business email:** roughly $6 to $12 per user per month. It's optional, but a name@yourbusiness.com address looks far more credible than a free webmail one.

Add those up and a basic site often lands somewhere around **$250 to $700 a year**.

## What costs depend on how your site is built?

This is where bills diverge. Ask about each of these:

- **Software updates and security patches.** If your site runs on a content management system with plugins, those need regular updates. Skipping them is how sites get hacked.
- **Backups.** Some hosts include them; others charge roughly $5 to $30 a month. Either way, you want daily or weekly copies you can restore.
- **Premium plugins or licenses.** Some tools renew yearly, anywhere from nothing to a few hundred dollars.
- **Content changes.** New services, new prices, a new team photo. Handle them yourself, or pay per task or by the hour (roughly $50 to $150 an hour is common).
- **Online store costs.** Payment processors take a percentage per sale, and some platforms add their own fees on top. That isn't maintenance, but it's a running cost you should budget for.
- **Performance and fixes.** A form that stops sending emails, a page that loads slowly, a broken link. Someone has to fix them.

## What's a realistic yearly budget?

Here's a rough guide. Treat it as a starting point, not a promise:

| Setup | Approximate yearly cost |
|---|---|
| Simple site, you make small edits yourself | $250 to $700 |
| Site plus business email and occasional paid help | $500 to $1,500 |
| Site with a maintenance or support plan | $1,200 to $4,800 |
| Online store with regular updates | $2,000 and up, plus transaction fees |

If a quote is far outside these ranges, ask what's behind it. There may be a good reason, or there may not.

## Do you pay more for a website builder or a custom site?

A do-it-yourself builder looks cheap: often around $15 to $40 a month for basic plans, more for online stores. The catch is that **you're renting**. Stop paying and the site typically disappears, and moving it somewhere else is usually hard or impossible.

A custom site costs more to build, but you can set it up so that the ongoing costs are only hosting and the domain. When a site is built in your name, you decide where it lives and who works on it. ## What happens if you skip maintenance?

Nothing dramatic at first, and that's what makes it risky. Over months, you may notice:

- Slower pages and a worse experience on phones.
- Contact forms or booking tools that quietly stop working, so you lose leads without knowing.
- Security holes in outdated software.
- Browser warnings, or search engines showing your site less often.
- A design that looks dated next to competitors.

A few hours of care a year is much cheaper than rebuilding after a hack or after months of lost inquiries.

## Can you maintain it yourself?

Yes, for simple jobs. You can usually handle:

1. Changing text, prices and hours.
2. Swapping photos.
3. Posting a new offer or announcement.
4. Checking monthly that your contact form still reaches your inbox.

Leave the technical work to a professional: updates to core software, security, backups, speed, and anything that touches payments or customer data. A good setup makes the first group easy for you and keeps the second group out of your way.

## What should you ask whoever builds your site?

Before you sign, get clear answers to these questions:

1. **Who owns the domain, hosting account and site files?** The answer should be you, in writing.
2. **What recurring costs will I have after launch, itemized?**
3. **Is there a mandatory monthly fee? What does it include?**
4. **Who handles updates, backups and security, and how often?**
5. **How do I request changes, and how fast do you respond?**
6. **If we stop working together, can I take the site with me?**

Vague answers are a warning sign. A good provider can list the costs on one page.

## What mistakes make maintenance more expensive?

- **Letting the domain expire.** Turn on auto-renew and use an email you actually check. Losing a domain can cost you your site, your email and your search presence.
- **Registering things in someone else's name.** If the designer owns your domain or hosting, you're negotiating when you want to leave.
- **Paying for features you don't use.** Review plans once a year.
- **Piling on plugins.** Every extra tool is something else to update and something else that can break.
- **Having no plan for changes.** If you don't know who to call, small problems wait until they're big.
